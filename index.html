You know how this goes.

You decide this is the week. New routine, clear plan, maybe a fresh notebook or a new app. And for a few days it works. You're up on time. You're doing the thing. You feel like someone who finally has it together.

Then an ordinary day shows up. You're tired, or busy, or you just don't feel like it. You skip once.

The next morning you think, well, I already missed yesterday. By the weekend the whole plan feels ruined. And somewhere in there, without ever really deciding to, you stop.

A few weeks later you're back at the start, promising yourself that next Monday will be different.

The missed day is rarely what hurts. What hurts is the story you tell yourself afterward.

Maybe I just don't have any discipline. Other people stick to things. Why can't I?

So you go looking for more self-discipline. You read the books about mental toughness. You watch the videos that make you feel ready to run through a wall, for about a day. You download a habit tracker and guard your streak like it's precious, right up until it breaks. Then the app goes quiet, and so do you.

None of that was wasted effort. But every one of those attempts aims at the same target: never missing. And you will miss. Everybody does. When the whole plan depends on a perfect run, the first bad day takes the plan down with it.

Here's the shift that changes things.

Plan the day after a miss before the miss ever happens.

Most plans are written for good days. They assume you'll have the time, the energy and the mood. On a bad day the plan has nothing to say, so you fill the silence with "I blew it."

There's also a very human habit working against you. Researchers call it the fresh-start effect: we're more likely to begin again at landmarks like a Monday, a new month or a new year. That pull is useful when you're starting something. After a slip it becomes a trap, because it turns one missed day into days or weeks of waiting for the "right" moment.

So the pattern you keep falling into isn't proof of a weak character. It's a design problem. And design problems can be fixed.

The Restart Rule

The Restart Rule is a four-part setup you build around one thing you've chosen to do. It assumes your willpower will run out sometimes, and it plans for that from day one.

  1. 1

    Set the Floor. Every commitment gets two versions: the full one for good days, and a floor so small it still counts on a bad one. Five minutes. One page. One line of code.

  2. 2

    Tie it to a Cue. Attach it to something you already do every day, like pouring your morning coffee, so you're not deciding from scratch each time.

  3. 3

    Plan the Miss. Decide now what the day after a miss looks like: the floor version, at the same cue, with no penalty and nothing to catch up on.

  4. 4

    Restart Tomorrow. No waiting for Monday. The day after a miss is your fresh start.

Two guardrails keep it honest. If you find yourself living on the floor for a couple of weeks, the rule nudges you back up to the full version, so the floor doesn't quietly turn into your ceiling. And if your misses keep landing in the same spot, you change the setup (a different cue, a smaller floor, a better time) instead of trying harder.

What it looks like on an ordinary Thursday.

You skipped yesterday. The old you would already be planning Monday's fresh start. Instead, you pour your coffee, glance at the card on the counter, and do the floor version. A few minutes, done. A streak app would call yesterday a failure. The Restart Rule calls today a restart, and today counts.

You stop counting streaks and start watching your Restart Gap: how many days it takes you to come back after a miss. When that number gets smaller, you're getting better at the part that actually matters.

Eventually I noticed the cycle was predictable. It broke the same way every time, right after a miss. That's a design problem, and design problems can be fixed.

So I quit planning for the perfect week and planned for the bad day instead. I assumed my willpower would run out, because it always had.

Every commitment got a floor, something so small it still counted on a rough day. A single rep. One line of code. I tied it to something I already did every day, like pouring my coffee. That's the Restart Rule. A missed day can still happen. The plan is that it stays one day.
